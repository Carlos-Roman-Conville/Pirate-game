# Goods: what ships carried and what pirates took (c. 1650–1730)

The starting goods list for the economy simulation (ALIGNMENT §10, fifth run-down). This file is research plus a
proposal. Rex decides which goods the game uses, what they are called and how they are classed.

**Marks.** **(source)** means the fact comes from the source IDs listed beside it (Part 4). **(proposed)** is this
session's suggestion for Rex to confirm or change. **(calc)** is arithmetic on sourced numbers, done here and not
found in the source. **open** means no source opened in this research gives the fact. Every cargo, weight and
price below is either sourced or open. Nothing is invented. **(unverified)** means a fact came from a search summary
or a weak page and should be checked against the original before it is used. **(estimate)** means a figure worked out
in this file from sourced inputs plus a stated assumption (Part 1j). It is a modelling number, not a historical record.

**Period.** Sources span 1650s–1730s. Rows that use evidence from outside the period say so ("late").

The pattern in these numbers (calc), counting by the pound:
- **Cochineal** (216–540d) is about 50–150 times muscovado sugar (about 3–4d).
- **Indigo and annatto** (42–108d) are 10–30 times sugar.
- **Sugar** is several times flour or biscuit.
- **Logwood and bar iron** (about 1.5–2d) are bulk goods, worth less by the pound than flour.

The same good cost very different amounts in different places. Tobacco brought 1–2d at a Virginia plantation and
15d in a London list. Logwood went for £5 a ton in trade goods at the camps and £25–30 sold from Jamaica. The sources
do not split that gap into freight, duty and profit, except for one duty figure: £4 15s per ton on logwood imported
into England (1701, CSP).

**Caveat.** Most of Rogers' English prices are retail purchases by colleges and households, and the OCR of his book
is rough (TR).

### 1i. Spoilage summary (source)

- **Spoils:**
  - flour and biscuit (weevils: SV);
  - salt meat, if badly packed or stored (SV, PY);
  - butter and cheese in the heat (L);
  - wine in the heat (L);
  - candles, which fuse together (L);
  - sugar, which drains in the hold (LAW, late);
  - live cargo (horses, turtle, people), which dies.
- **Does not spoil:** salt, metals, woods, shell, coin.
- **Open:** molasses and rum leakage rates, gunpowder damp, and the life of cotton, cloth and tobacco at sea.

### 1j. Cask estimates: sugar, molasses, rum (estimate)

Rex asked for estimates where the sources give no fixed size (2026-10-28). Each one is the cask's volume times the
density of what it holds, plus the weight of an oak cask. The inputs are sourced; the assumptions are listed. The
results are modelling numbers, not period records.

**Inputs (source):**
- **Cask volumes,** in wine gallons of 231 cubic inches, 3.785 litres (WC): hogshead 63 gal (238 L), puncheon 84 gal
  (318 L), tierce 42 gal (159 L), barrel 31.5 gal (119 L). Rum puncheon "about 130 gallons" (PU; which gallon is not
  stated). Rundlet 12–18½ gal (PY).
- **A large hogshead's outside limits:** the 1730 Virginia Act allowed staves up to 48 in long and heads up to 30 in
  across (VA30). That rule is for tobacco; using it for sugar is an assumption.
- **Densities:**
  - Molasses: specific gravity 1.41, "equivalent to 11.75 lb/gallon" at 79.5° Brix (KER).
  - Raw cane sugar: 960 kg/m³ in bulk; granulated sugar 800 kg/m³ (ETB). Brown sugar about 721 kg/m³, but from a
    weak second-hand page.
  - Spirit: density 0.946 at 40.6% alcohol by volume (CAN); about 0.898 for 57% alcohol by weight at 20 °C (NIST).
    Period rum strength is open, so rum is taken as 0.90–0.95 kg/L.
  - Oak: white oak "weighs about 770 kg/m³ when dried" (TS).
- **Customs tare for sugar:** 12 lb in every 112 lb (US Treasury, 1791). Late, and unverified: the letters could not
  be opened.

**Assumptions (estimate):**
- Staves and heads ¾–1 in thick (19–25 mm).
- The large cask is 46 in long inside, with a bilge (widest point) of 34–36 in.
- Hoops (wood or iron) are not counted, so the cask weights below are low.
- Casks are full. How full casks were really filled is open.
- Modern molasses density stands in for period molasses.

**Results (estimate):**

| Cask | Volume | Contents, net | Cask (oak, est.) | Gross |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sugar hogshead, small (wine-hogshead size) | 238 L, 63 gal | 420–505 lb (density 0.80–0.96) | 70–90 lb | about 490–595 lb |
| Sugar hogshead, large (1730 Act limits) | about 630–690 L (formula below) | 1,120–1,460 lb | 130–170 lb | about 1,250–1,630 lb |
| Molasses hogshead | 238 L, 63 gal | about 740 lb (11.75 lb/gal) | 70–90 lb | about 810–830 lb |

**How each line is worked out:**
- **Large cask volume:** the gauger's cask rule V = πh(2D² + d²)/12, with h = 46 in, D = 34–36 in at the bilge, and
  d = 30 in at the head. This gives 38,700–42,100 cubic inches.
- **Cask weight:** the outer surface of a cylinder of the same volume times the stave thickness times 770 kg/m³. That
  is about 2.1 m² for the small cask and 4.0 m² for the large one.

**Checks against the sources:**
- **Small sugar hogshead:** two "Barbadas" hogsheads held "nine hundred weight" in the 17th century (ML). That is 450
  lb each at 100 lb a hundredweight, or 504 lb at 112 lb. It sits inside the small cask's 420–505 lb, so the small
  hogshead fits the 17th-century evidence.
- **Bristol, 1752:** a hogshead of about 272 kg, roughly 600 lb (DB1), sits between the two sizes.
- **Later average:** 1,792 lb (an 18th–19th century average, from Wikipedia without a citation) is heavier than even
  the large estimate. Sugar hogsheads grew over the century.
- **Cask weight:** the estimated 70–90 lb on 420–505 lb of sugar is about 14–18% of the net weight. The 1791 customs
  rule is about 11%, so the estimate is in the same range, a little high.

**Working figures for the game (proposed).** One hogshead is 63 gallons for all three goods in 1650–1730:
- sugar about 450 lb net, about 530 lb gross;
- molasses about 740 lb net, about 820 lb gross;
- rum about 490 lb net, about 570 lb gross.

Rex can instead pick the large hogshead for sugar if the game is set later, or let cask size grow over the game's
timeline. Either is his choice (Open for Rex, item 8).

## 2. What pirates took most (source)

Ranked by how often each kind of good appears in about 40 incidents from Johnson's *General History* (1724, which
includes trial records) and Exquemelin (1678/1684), with archaeology from two wrecks. The records give no frequency
tables, so the ranking is a count of the incidents that were read, not a full survey (J, E).

Johnson says it himself: the Caribbean offers pirates "Booties of Provision, Cloathing, and Naval-Stores, and
sometimes Money" (J, Introduction).

1. **Provisions and drink.** These appear in almost every incident. Examples:
   - Teach took "120 Barrels of Flower" (1717).
   - Bonnet took "ten or twelve Barrels of Pork, and about 400 Weight of Bread", plus rum and molasses (1718).
   - Roberts took 10 casks of oatmeal and 6 of beef (c. 1720).
   - Lowther took thirty casks of brandy, five hogsheads of wine (1721) and fourteen hogsheads of rum (1722).
   - Low took 300 gallons of brandy (1722).
   - Exquemelin's buccaneers raided for "flour, bread, pork, brandy, wines". (J ch. III, IV, IX, XII, XIII; E ch. VII)
2. **Money, plate, gold dust.** The best-known hauls are Roberts' 40,000 moidores (1720), 260,000 pieces of eight at
   Maracaibo (1660s) and gold dust off Africa. In ordinary prizes the money is often small and comes after the stores.
   (J ch. VIII, IX; E ch. VII)
3. **Naval stores and ship's gear.** Cables, hawsers, rope, sails, canvas, masts, yards and the carpenter's stores.
   At the 1722 Cape Coast trial, witnesses named who took what from the King Solomon: the boatswain "two Cables, and
   several Coils of Rope", the sail-maker the sails and canvas, the cooper the cask and tools. Roberts took "Sails,
   Guns, Powder, Cordage" from the Samuel (1720). (J ch. IX, XIII)
4. **Powder, guns and small arms.** Examples: five barrels of powder (Roberts); six quarter-casks of powder and two guns
   (Low); "7,000 weight of powder, a great number of muskets" (L'Olonnais). Guns were also taken to arm the pirates'
   own ships. (J ch. IX, XIII; E ch. VII)
5. **Cloth, clothing and dry goods.** "Abundance of Cloaths", bales of goods, chintzes, silk and linen.
   (J ch. IX, XII, XIII; E ch. VII)
6. **People with trades.** Carpenters and surgeons were forced aboard (Phillips 1724; the Elizabeth's surgeon), and
   sailors joined or were pressed. (J ch. IX, XV)
7. **Medicine and surgical instruments.** Examples:
   - Blackbeard's Charleston medicine chest (1718, valued at £300–400).
   - The King Solomon's surgeon's "capital Instruments, some Medicines".
   - The Queen Anne's Revenge wreck held a syringe, a clyster pump and a mortar and pestle.
   - Exquemelin's articles set aside 200–250 pieces of eight for the surgeon's chest.
   (J ch. III, IX; E ch. V; QAR)
8. **Enslaved people.** Pirates took them as cargo (140 from the Morrice in 1719; 40 from the Greyhound in 1721) and
   sold them or kept them. (J ch. VIII, XII)
9. **Bulk cargo** (sugar, cocoa, tobacco, indigo, cotton). Taken when the prize could be sold or condemned in a port.
   Teach's French prize gave the North Carolina governor "sixty Hogsheads of Sugar"; his crew were found with 25
   hogsheads of sugar, 145 bags of cocoa, a barrel of indigo and a bale of cotton. (J ch. III, IX; E ch. VII)
10. **The ship itself.** Roberts swapped his ship for the Onslow and renamed her the Royal Fortune (1721). Bellamy took
    the Whydah (1717). (J ch. IX; RMG; WK, unverified)

**Thrown away or destroyed.** Roberts' men opened bales and "threw them over-board" (1720), and did the same on the
Guinea coast (1721). Low cut away a sloop's rigging and sails so news of him would not spread. Ships were burned,
some for revenge (Teach and the Protestant Caesar), some to deny them to others (Trepassey, 1720). (J ch. III, IX,
XIII)

**For the game (proposed).** The records suggest what a pirate wants from a prize: food, drink, cordage, canvas,
powder, medicine and hands come before cargo to sell. Bulk cash crops are worth something only with a port that will
buy them. Rex decides whether the economy models this.

## 3. The three types, as proposed

Rex's classes (ALIGNMENT §10): **base** goods every settlement needs, with steady demand; **specialty** goods only a
region with the knowledge can make, wanted by some towns and not others; **cultural** goods valued fully only by their
own civilisation. Every Type cell in Part 1 is **(proposed)**. The reasoning behind the proposal:

- **Base (proposed).** Food and drink (flour, biscuit, salt meat and fish, pease, oatmeal, turtle, rum, molasses,
  lime juice, salt). Building and ship goods (lumber, staves, shingles, cordage, sailcloth, candles). Plain cloth,
  clothing, iron and iron tools, small wares, arms, powder and shot, medicine. Hides, tallow, cotton. Horses. The
  sources show every colony importing these and pirates taking them first.
- **Specialty (proposed).** Goods tied to one region's crop, ground or skill: sugar (all three grades), indigo,
  annatto, ginger, pimento, logwood and the other dyewoods, mahogany, tortoise shell, tobacco, rice, naval stores,
  deerskins, cochineal, pearls, silver, ivory, Asian textiles.
- **Cultural (proposed).** Goods one people valued above what others would pay:
  - wine, brandy, beer, cheese and butter (European tastes);
  - cacao (a Spanish taste first);
  - boucan (the hunters' own food);
  - cowries, manillas and voyage iron (currency goods on the West African coast).
  The sources show where these went, but not that others valued them less. That part is a judgement, not a sourced
  fact.

## 3c. One flat skill list (Rex, 2026-10-28)

**The rule (Rex).**
- **One skill list, no layers.** There are no professions and no profession levels, and trade skills are not kept
  apart from crew skills. The withdrawn twelve-profession grouping was in an earlier version of this part.
- **Skills grow by use.** Each skill levels independently when a character does the work: catching a fish raises
  Fishing, making rum raises Distilling.
- **One value per skill per character, shared everywhere.** The same skill counts on the ship, in a town job, in
  exploration, in crafting and later in combat. Smithing learned in town stays when the smith joins the crew.
- **When a new activity gets its own skill:** only if it deserves its own progression. Otherwise it uses an existing
  skill.

Combat classes and magic disciplines are a later topic, not covered here.

which equipment they use**. Gathering a thing and processing it are separate skills when the expertise differs.

**38 skills, 32 new.** The wood split (Rex, 2026-10-28), checked against the record (`research-wood-trades.md`):
three trades stand apart from Carpentry because Campbell 1747 and the companies treat them so: **Shipwrighting**
(Shipwrights' Company 1605 and 1612; Campbell's ship-builder, boat-builder and block-maker; Bermuda's cedar sloops
from the mid-1600s), **Cabinetmaking** (Campbell's cabinet-maker and carver, apart from the joiner; "Cabanittmakers"
at Port Royal 1688, second-hand), and **Millwrighting** (Campbell's mill-wright and wheeler; Ligon asked Barbados for
"Mill-wrights, Wheel-wrights"; the sugar mill is the heart of a sugar island). Folded in on the evidence: the joiner
into Carpentry (Campbell: "generally the same Person with the Carpenter"; the 1630–80 dispute was two companies over
one trade), the sawyer into Carpentry's Novice rung (Ligon's "three whip-Sawes ... in a Frame or Pit"), the caulker
and mast-maker into Shipwrighting (no separate entries found), and the turner into Cooperage (Campbell: "the common
Turner is generally a Cooper"). The tar burner and the collier were found as no occupation at all and stay inside
Woodcutting. The wet/dry cooper split is reported but the research could not open a page confirming it (unverified).

**35 before the wood split.** Rex's metal split (2026-10-28, from his notes), checked against the record
(`research-metal-trades.md`, shared notes): the six trades his notes name were real, separate London companies with
their own apprenticeships: Blacksmiths (1571), Gunmakers (1637, with proof of barrels), Cutlers (1416), Founders
(1614; the King's Gunfounder from 1615), Goldsmiths, Pewterers (by 1348). Campbell's *London Tradesman* (1747)
gives each its own entry. So **Smithing becomes Blacksmithing** and four skills stand beside it: Gunsmithing,
Bladesmithing, Founding, Precious Metalworking. Not a skill: **Fine Metalworking**. Nothing opened shows a whitesmith
as a separate trade, and Campbell's index says "Lock-smith, see Smith", so locks and keys sit at Blacksmithing
Expert. Also folded into Blacksmithing on Campbell's word: the anchor-smith ("a laborious and profitable Branch of
the Smith Trade"), the farrier (a company from 1674, but "a compound of the smith and" the horse-doctor), and the
nailer (a Midlands by-employment). The armourer's trade was "merely nominal" by 1747; the ship's armourer repaired
arms, so he sits at Gunsmithing Novice. The pewterer casts in a mould, so pewter sits in Founding; 33 pewter
pieces with London marks of 1689–1712 came off the Queen Anne's Revenge (QAR).

What the colonies could do: Port Royal had blacksmiths, gun and sword smiths, pewterers, silversmiths and
goldsmiths. It had no steel furnace (none in the English colonies before the 1720s) and no cannon foundry
(none before 1730), and sword blades "come mostly from abroad" (Campbell). So steel, blades and great guns are
off-map; the town can forge, mount, repair and cast small things.

**31 before the metal split.** Powder Making and Chemical Refining added (Rex, 2026-10-28): the powder-maker and the
saltpetre-maker were trades of their own, apart from gunsmith, smith and gunner. Gunpowder needs all three of
saltpetre (Chemical Refining, from nitre earth), refined sulphur (Chemical Refining, from Mining's brimstone) and
charcoal (kept inside Woodcutting). No Caribbean powder mill is in the sources, so the town imports powder until it
recruits a powder-maker. Recipes stay abstract: no manufacturing procedure is modelled.

**29 before them.** Rex's third split (2026-10-28, from his notes): Spinning, Weaving, Ropemaking and Tailoring
are four trades, not one; Shoemaking is its own trade after Tanning; Leatherworking (added the same day) keeps the

**Rex's recipe rule (2026-10-28):** every recipe needs **materials + equipment + a minimum skill band**. Skill never
creates a material: a master spinner cannot spin silk without silk, a master ropemaker still needs hemp and a
ropewalk. A ropewalk may take raw fibre or a spinner's yarn; the dashboard shows that as "needs one of". Rex's second split (2026-10-28, from his notes): Cooking keeps meals only; Milling, Baking,
Butchering, Preserving, Dairying and Waxworking are skills of their own, and Tanning is kept apart from Butchering.

## 3d. Occupations: labels over the skills (Rex, 2026-10-28; sourced)

**The rule (Rex).** An occupation is a label that groups the skills a person of that calling practised. It levels
nothing and grants nothing; the skills do. A character may carry two or more. It is for organising the roster and
the trees now, and later for how the player reads a character ("a Mariner and Cooper").

**What the period did (source: `research-occupations.md`, shared notes folder; the dashboard's Occupations panel
carries the evidence per word).**
- Wills and indentures style a person by **one word**: Merchant (the commonest at Port Royal, 19 of 100), Mariner,
  Planter, Gentleman, Chirurgeon, Cooper, Carpenter, Apothecary, Taylor, Vintner, Victualler, Cordwainer,
  Chandler, Distiller, Sugar-baker, Blacksmith, Joiner, Goldsmith.
- **Status words sat in the same slot** as trades: Gentleman, Esquire, Yeoman, Husbandman, Labourer, Servant, and
  militia Captain or Colonel. A man could be "maltster" in his will and "gentleman" on his memorial.
- **Two words were real but rare:** under 5% of mariners, nearly always sea + shipboard craft: "Mariner and Cooper"
  (eight wills 1669–1725), "Mariner and Shipwright", "Mariner and Caulker", "Mariner and Surgeon", "Mariner and
  Gunner", "Merchant, Commander". "Planter and Merchant" as a formula: none found. "Barber Chirurgeon" (306 wills)
  is the one compound trade.
- **By-employments** in an English market town (Spalding, 1642): a husbandman who was a musician, a shoemaker who
  was also a weaver, butchers who were graziers.
- **"Alchemist" was never an occupation:** 0 wills, against 40 "Chymist" or "Chemist" (1662–1760); a 1728 record
  calls a transmuter "a pretended alchimist". The game's label for Rex's "Alchemist" group is **Chymist**.
- **A ship's company** (Smith, 1627; Johnson's trial, 1722): officers (Master, Mates, Boatswain, Gunner,
  Quarter-master), the exempted tradesmen (Chirurgeon, Carpenter, Cooper, Sailmaker, Armourer, Cook, Musicians),
  and the seamen (Sailers, the able men; Younkers, the foremast men; Boys).
- **A plantation** (Ligon, 1657): the Overseer, the fire-man at the furnaces, the smith's forge, the carpenter's and
  joiner's houses, "the Apothecary of the Plantation, which we call Doctor". Boiler and distiller as job titles:
  open for 1657.

**The list (proposed, 31 period words)** is in `skill-tree.json` under `occupations` and on the dashboard: each word
with the skills it groups (goods skills and `STATS.md` skills alike), its evidence and date, and a note. The grouping
is Rex's organising layer under period names; Rex's category names map as Agriculturalist → Husbandman, Food Artisan
→ Victualler, Alchemist → Chymist, Textile Artisan → Weaver and Taylor, Metalworker → Smith, Woodworker → Carpenter
and Cooper, Military Specialist → Soldier, Merchant/Diplomat → Merchant. Perfumer has no period source.
