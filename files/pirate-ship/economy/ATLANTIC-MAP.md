# The Atlantic map: the base game's locales (Rex, 2026-10-29)

Rex's decision: the base game is the Atlantic world, with about 70 locales in six regions, each a node in the
economy and a place in the story. This file is the economy's record of them: what each was in 1650–1730, what it
made and wanted, what pirates did there, and whether the record supports it. Marks as in GOODS.md: **(source)**,
**(proposed)**, **open**. Verdicts: **fits** / **fits with a caveat** (dated) / **outside the period** /
**plausible adaptation** (nothing documented, nothing against) / **nothing found**.

The town keeper's `town/` files own the design of a locale (its look, people and quests); the twelve locales in
ALIGNMENT §10 predate this list and need reconciling with it (Open for Rex, 1). Research notes are in the shared
folder: `map-caribbean-1.md`, `map-caribbean-2.md`, `map-caribbean-small.md`, `map-mainland-europe.md`, and for
Africa `research-africa-kongo-angola.md`, `research-africa-ouidah.md`, `research-africa-senegambia-calabar.md`.
The Caribbean and small-settlement notes lean on Wikipedia pages opened as pointers, plus Johnson 1724 and museum
pages; the African notes reach period texts (Phillips 1694, Bosman 1705, Barbot, Labat 1728, Johnson, Latham).

## 3. The African coast: five settings, one spectrum (source)

Rex's purpose: five locales that show the breadth of African relationships to the Atlantic slave trade, from
flagrant to fighting, with the trickery in between, each a distinct society and not a moral label on a people.
What the period record supports, port by port. Each has a timeline, money, goods and "what a ship could do" in
its research note.

| | Senegambia (Saint-Louis, Gorée, the river) | Kongo coast (Mpinda, Soyo) | Ndongo and Matamba (Luanda's hinterland) | Hueda (Ouidah, Savi) | Old Calabar (the Cross River) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Place on the spectrum** | **opposition, in the period:** Nasir al-Din's movement 1673–77 demanded the kings "stop enslaving their own subjects" and refused to sell Muslims to Christians; it overthrew the satigi, damel and brak and was crushed by 1677 with French slave-raiding flotillas from Saint-Louis. After it, the ceddo regimes sold again | **regulation lost:** the early kings separated foreign captives (saleable) from the free-born (protected); by the civil wars (1665–1709) "all Kongos became subject to sale", kings and princes selling, 30,000 sold after Pedro IV's 1709 victory. Afonso's 1526 protest is background only; no royal protest in 1650–1730 was found | **resistance and participation at once:** Njinga harboured and armed runaways from the Portuguese ("her kingdom 'had none'"), and sold war captives, 13,000 a year to the Dutch for guns; in 1656 she accepted a Portuguese slave market in her capital. Her successors contracted slaves to Portugal (200 a year, 1683) | **the merchant kingdom:** the king sold as state policy: customs of "six slaves value in cowries", "the king's slaves ... first offer'd to sale", "we must not refuse them" (Phillips 1694); about 1,000 a month (Bosman); Huffon's 1706 open port, trade free to all nations; conquered by Dahomey 1727 | **the organised trade:** willing Efik selling from 1668 ("prisoners taken in war"), a crowded river by 1700, the copper bar as money, comey about 10%, 75 people and 11,400 yams in one ship's accounts (1720). No cleric or ruler against the trade; resistance on record is to European seizure and to pirates |
| **Who sells, who is sold** | kings sold war captives and, in the 1670s, their own subjects; Barbot: families sold members, kings sold subjects "for minor offences"; most captives from upriver; the gum trade with the Moors never involved slaves | war captives of the Soyo–Kongo–Ngoyo wars; civil-war captives "along with their families and supporters"; much shipped through Loango and Cabinda | the feiras (interior markets) and pombeiros deliver war captives, tribute slaves and purchases; Luanda ships them to Bahia, Pernambuco, Rio | the king's stock first, then the "cappasheirs" each "according to his degree"; interior wars and Dahomey's raids supply; the sellers shave captives "to hide age" | Efik middlemen between the ships and inland suppliers; lower-river captives first, then raiding, then inland purchase |
| **Money and customs** | the iron bar (slaves 12–20 bars; 8 hides a bar); the brak's and damel's fees on every boatload; hide duty 16%; gum customs in piastres | nzimbu shells and libongo cloth (cloth from 1651; a failed copper coin 1694) | nzimbu, libongo and makuta, réis | cowries by weight (about 100 lb a man in 1694); the king's customs; prices fixed "before the king", no bargaining | the copper bar (38–48 a man); comey to a list of "kings" and "dukes"; ransoms in the accounts |
| **Goods beyond people** | hides ("the main commodity", Barbot), gum arabic, Bambuk gold, ivory, wax, millet | ivory, copper, raffia cloth, redwood, salt | salt of Kisama, ivory, wax; slaves dominate | provisions, palm oil, a little gold dust, some cloth and ivory at Allada | yams by the thousand, fish, plantains, pepper, palm oil, ivory, barwood |
| **Institutions** | the marabouts and the Torodbe; the ceddo warrior-slaves of the damel; the signares and grumetes of the French posts | the elected Catholic prince of Soyo; the Capuchin missions; Kimpa Vita's Antonian movement 1704–06 (religious and reunifying; its "antislavery" is uncited) | the queen's court; the Imbangala; a permanent Portuguese representative at court from 1656 | the king and his cappasheirs; the python cult of Dangbe (the snake-house); European forts of four nations | the trading houses; Ekpe, the trust system and pawning are documented after 1710 and mainly mid-century: later than the game, mark as such |
| **Europeans present** | French at Saint-Louis (1659) and Gorée (1677); English at James Island | Dutch and English traders; Portuguese at Luanda next door | Portugal's governor at Luanda; Dutch 1641–48 | Dutch post c. 1670, English fort 1680s, French 1704, Portuguese 1721 | English and Bristol slavers; no fort |
| **What a pirate could do (source)** | Davis took Gambia Castle 1719; Roberts took the French guard ships off Senegal 1721 (J) | trade with Soyo; be fought: Soyo had Dutch guns | trade only at Luanda, a Portuguese fortress | Roberts ransomed eleven ships for 8 lb of gold dust each (January 1722) and burned the Porcupine with about 80 people aboard | careen ("the most suitable along the whole Coast", J); be refused: the town turned out 2,000 men and would not trade with Roberts; take Bristol ships in the river |
| **Story identity (Rex's labels)** | the Sanctuary Coast | the Compromised Kingdom | the Defiant Kingdom | the Merchant Kingdom | the Cruel Port |
| **Where the fiction must declare itself** | the Almamy's 1787 ban is after the period: use Nasir al-Din instead | a 1690s king cannot speak Afonso's 1526 words; Kimpa Vita's abolitionism is not in the sources | the cannibal and lover legends (Cavazzi, Dapper) are unproven; both halves of her record are true | Agaja's "abolitionist" motive for 1727 is rejected by the scholarship; his motives were arms, money, gold and the sea | the rulers profited; nothing shows they enjoyed cruelty. A faction that glorifies domination is Rex's invention, to be marked so |

**The spectrum, read across (proposed).** Opposition that lost (Senegambia) → protection that collapsed (Kongo) →
defiance that also sold (Matamba) → trade as the state (Hueda) → trade as the whole society (Calabar). Each
had merchants, dissenters, rulers and captives; the research notes name them where the record does.

**Goods these five add to GOODS.md:** none new. Gum, hides, wax, ivory, gold, palm oil, yams, copper, raffia,
redwood, kola, salt and the coast's money are rows 82–94 and 1g already.

## 5. How the data carries the map

`atlantic-map.json` (this folder) lists every node with its region, origin, tier (major / minor / candidate),
verdict and the goods it is known to send out and take in (ids from `goods.json`). `build-economy-data.py` writes
`data/pirate-ship/economy/ports.json` from it: a node per locale, the five per-settlement numbers (stock,
consumption per day, target reserve, production per day, base price) left null for the balance lane, and
`produces` / `consumes` lists as the research gives them. Goods a node is known to make come from its recipes'
region and origin; the rest is open until the balance lane fills it.
